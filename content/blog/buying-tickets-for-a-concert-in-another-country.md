Plenty of the biggest tours on TourTicketCompare cross borders. [Harry Styles](/artists/harry-styles) has dates listed in the United States, Canada, Australia, Ireland, Spain and the UK; [Karol G](/artists/karol-g) in the United States and across Europe; [Oasis](/artists/oasis) in Scotland, England, Ireland, Germany, Spain and the Netherlands; and [BTS](/artists/bts) in Australia. For some fans the nearest date, or the only one left, is in another country. Buying a ticket for that show works much like buying one at home, with a handful of extra checks that are easy to miss.

## Read every time in the right zone

Two different clocks matter: the on-sale time and the show time. An on-sale announced for 10am usually means 10am where the venue is, not where you are. Convert it before the day, and check whether either country changes its clocks between now and then. The [onsale preparation guide](/guides/how-to-prepare-for-a-ticket-onsale) covers the rest of the routine.

The show date needs the same care once travel is involved. A late show that ends after midnight can matter for the last train, the hotel booking and the flight home.

## Know which ticket site covers that country

Large ticket sites often run separate country sites, and an account on one does not always carry over to another. Before the sale, find out which site the venue or tour names for that country, create the account in advance if you need one, and confirm that it accepts your card and billing address. Mobile tickets can also depend on the ticket site's app for that region, so check that you can install and sign in to it.

If the ticket is transfer-locked or tied to a name, make sure the name on the order matches the identification you will carry. Some venues ask to see it at the gate.

## Compare totals in one currency

A total in euros, pounds or Australian dollars is not comparable with one in US dollars until both are converted (the [currency converter](/currency-converter) does this with reference rates), and your card may add its own conversion charge on top. Compare final checkout totals for the same number of tickets, then work out what your card will actually charge. The [fees guide](/guides/concert-ticket-fees-explained) lists the charges that tend to appear late.

Fee rules differ by country as well. In the United States, the FTC's rule on unfair or deceptive fees requires mandatory fees to be included in the upfront price of live-event tickets, although taxes, shipping and optional add-ons can still be shown separately. Other countries set their own rules, so read the order summary rather than assuming the first price shown is the one you pay.

## Resale rules follow the venue, not you

Resale rules are set where the event takes place. In the UK, for example, section 90 of the Consumer Rights Act 2015 requires a ticket resold through a secondary ticketing platform to come with the information needed to identify the seat or standing area, any restriction on who may use it, and its face value. That protection applies to UK events wherever the buyer lives. Other countries restrict resale differently, and some tours add their own terms, so read the terms attached to the specific ticket before buying it second-hand.

## Book travel you can change

Tickets can arrive late, and shows can be rescheduled. Until the ticket is in your account and the date is settled, flexible or refundable travel is worth more than it would be for a local show. If a date moves, the [postponement and cancellation guide](/guides/what-to-do-if-a-concert-is-postponed-or-cancelled) explains what to check first.
