"No tickets available" on Ticketmaster means none are on sale there at that moment, for that search. It is not always final. Ticketmaster says venues release held seats as the show gets closer, and orders that fail are put back on sale, so checking again does work sometimes. Your other routes are Ticketmaster's own resale listings, and resale marketplaces such as SeatGeek and Vivid Seats, where prices are set by sellers. Which one makes sense depends on whether the tour restricts resale, how close the show is, and how much above face value you are willing to pay.

## First, check it is not your search

Ticketmaster's UK help page lists three reasons for the message: the search filters are too narrow, the tickets sold quickly, or a presale has ended and the general sale has not started. Before anything else:

- Clear price, quantity and section filters, and try one ticket instead of four. Single seats often remain when pairs do not.
- Check the date and venue. Multi-night runs have several near-identical events.
- If a presale just closed, note when the general sale opens and come back then.

## Why more tickets can appear later

Ticketmaster's help centre gives several reasons tickets come back:

- The venue releases tickets it held back, for example seats the artist or production no longer needs.
- Once the stage is built, the venue knows which seats are not obstructed and puts more on sale.
- An order fails because a card is declined and the buyer cannot be reached.
- A buyer breaks the ticket limits or terms, and their tickets are cancelled and released.

Ticketmaster also says it cannot promise more tickets will come. Release timing is not announced, so the practical approach is to check at a few set times, such as the week of the show and the day before, rather than refreshing all day.

## Ticketmaster's own resale

On many events Ticketmaster shows resale listings next to its standard tickets, sold by fans who bought earlier. How much those cost depends on the tour.

Some artists use Face Value Exchange. On those tours Ticketmaster sets every resale listing at what the seller originally paid, including fees and taxes, so you pay face value. Ticketmaster's blog says tickets on Face Value Exchange tours are mobile-only and restricted from transfer, which is how the price cap holds. On other tours, Ticketmaster resale prices are set by sellers and can be well above face value.

## If the tour restricts transfers

This is the check most people skip. When a tour locks tickets to the buyer's account or restricts transfer, a listing on another site may not be deliverable in the normal way. Read the event's ticket terms on Ticketmaster before you buy elsewhere. If they say tickets cannot be transferred, Ticketmaster's own resale is the only route that matches them.

## Buying on a resale site

When transfers are allowed, resale marketplaces are a normal way to buy after the primary sale runs out. They are not a scam by default, but the rules differ from a box-office purchase:

- **The seller sets the price.** It can be above or below face value, and it moves as the show gets closer. There is no reliable day when prices drop; see [when is the best time to buy concert tickets](/guides/when-is-the-best-time-to-buy-concert-tickets).
- **Compare totals, not headline prices.** Take each order to the final review screen and compare the same section and row. [How to compare concert ticket prices](/guides/how-to-compare-concert-ticket-prices) has a method.
- **Read the guarantee.** Established marketplaces back orders against tickets that do not arrive or do not scan. A postponed show is usually not refunded on resale. See [is SeatGeek legit](/guides/is-seatgeek-legit) and [is Vivid Seats legit](/guides/is-vivid-seats-legit).
- **Expect late delivery.** Resale tickets often arrive in the last day or two before the show. That is normal, and covered by the guarantee if they never come.

Avoid buying from strangers on social media or by bank transfer. Payment methods with no buyer protection are where most real ticket fraud happens. [How to avoid ticket scams](/guides/how-to-avoid-ticket-scams) covers the warning signs.

## A simple plan

1. Clear your filters and recheck Ticketmaster, including single seats.
2. Check Ticketmaster's resale listings, and whether the tour uses Face Value Exchange or restricts transfers.
3. If transfers are allowed, compare the same seats on one or two resale marketplaces on the checkout total.
4. Set a ceiling you would pay, and check back near the show if nothing fits it.

TourTicketCompare lists each upcoming date with its checked links to Ticketmaster and resale sites. Ticketmaster links are plain links; resale sites may pay the site a commission, which does not change your price.

## FAQ

**Does Ticketmaster release more tickets?**

Sometimes. Ticketmaster says venues can release held seats as the show approaches, and that tickets from failed or cancelled orders go back on sale. It does not guarantee it, and timing is not announced.

**What does "Tickets not currently available" mean on Ticketmaster?**

Ticketmaster's UK help page gives three causes: filters that are too narrow, tickets that sold quickly, or a presale that has ended before the general sale opens.

**Is Ticketmaster resale at face value?**

Only on tours that use Face Value Exchange, where resale listings are fixed at what the seller paid, including fees. On other tours the seller sets the resale price.

**Is it safe to buy resale tickets from another site?**

Buying on an established marketplace with a published buyer guarantee is a normal, protected purchase. Check the event's transfer terms first, compare the full total, and never pay a private seller by bank transfer.

**Do resale prices go down closer to the concert?**

They can go either way. Resale prices follow seller decisions and demand, and no day reliably brings them down.
